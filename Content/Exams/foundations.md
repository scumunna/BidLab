:::q Arithmetic & Rates
question: A line item served 250,000 impressions and recorded 1,500 clicks. What was its click-through rate?
- 6%
- 0.6%
- 0.06%
- 1.5%
answer: 1
explain: CTR is clicks divided by impressions, 1,500 divided by 250,000 equals 0.006, which is 0.6 percent. A rate is just one count over another, here clicks per impression.
:::

:::q Algebra & Functions
question: You know your spend was 5,000 dollars and your CPC was 2 dollars. How many clicks did you buy?
- 10,000 clicks
- 1,000 clicks
- 2,500 clicks
- 250 clicks
answer: 2
explain: Spend equals CPC times clicks, so clicks equals spend divided by CPC, 5,000 divided by 2 equals 2,500. Isolate the unknown by dividing both sides by the known unit price.

:::q Algebra & Functions
question: Your CPM is 8 dollars and your CTR is 0.25%. Using CPC equals CPM divided by 1,000 times CTR, what is the implied CPC?
- 2 dollars
- 3.20 dollars
- 32 dollars
- 0.32 dollars
answer: 1
explain: CPC equals 8 divided by the product of 1,000 and 0.0025, which is 8 divided by 2.5 equals 3.20 dollars. One thousand impressions cost 8 dollars and yield 2.5 clicks, so each click costs 3.20 dollars.
:::

:::q Algebra & Functions
question: A straight line written as conversions equals slope times spend plus a starting value describes which kind of relationship?
- Saturating, where each extra dollar adds less than the last
- Exponential, where output grows by a constant percentage
- Linear, where each extra dollar adds the same amount of output
- Random, with no fixed relationship
answer: 2
explain: A straight line has a constant slope, so every additional unit of input adds exactly the same amount to the output. That constant amount per unit is the definition of a linear function, unlike a saturating curve that bends toward a ceiling.
:::

:::q Optimization
question: Winning prices are normally distributed with a mean of 7 dollars and a standard deviation of 1 dollar. Planning to the average alone is risky mainly because:
- The mean ignores the spread, so a long tail of expensive wins can quietly eat the budget
- The mean is always wrong for skewed data
- A normal curve has no tails
- The standard deviation is irrelevant once you know the mean
answer: 0
explain: An average tells you where the data centers but not how far it strays. Optimizing to the mean while ignoring the spread misses the tails, where a few very expensive wins or very high-value conversions can dominate the actual result.
:::

:::q Optimization
question: Under a fixed budget, the next dollar in channel A would return 2 dollars and the next dollar in channel B would return 4 dollars. What should you do?
- Leave it, since both channels are profitable
- Move budget from B to A to balance the spend evenly
- Split every new dollar evenly between A and B
- Move budget from A to B until their marginal returns are equal
answer: 3
explain: The next dollar does more in B than in A, so shifting from A to B raises the total outcome. Keep moving until the marginal returns equalize, which is the equal-marginal condition for the optimum. Equal marginal value, not equal spend, defines the best allocation.
:::

:::q Arithmetic & Rates

:::q Probability & Statistics
question: Winning prices are normally distributed with a mean of 6 dollars and a standard deviation of 1.50 dollars. About what share of wins cost between 4.50 and 7.50 dollars?
- About 95%
- About 68%
- About 50%
- About 99%
answer: 1
explain: The range from 4.50 to 7.50 dollars is one standard deviation on each side of the mean, since 6 minus 1.50 is 4.50 and 6 plus 1.50 is 7.50. For a normal curve, about 68 percent of values fall within one standard deviation of the mean.
