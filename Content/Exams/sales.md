:::q Deals & Pricing
question: A line item delivered 750,000 impressions for a media cost of 6,000 dollars. What is the CPM?
- 0.80 dollars
- 8 dollars
- 12.50 dollars
- 80 dollars
answer: 1
explain: CPM is cost per thousand impressions: 6,000 divided by 750,000, times 1,000, equals 8 dollars. CPM, CPC, and CPA are just different ways to slice the same delivery.
:::

:::q Deals & Pricing
question: A buyer commits to a fixed volume of impressions at a fixed CPM, and the publisher reserves that inventory. Which deal type is this?
- A private marketplace auction
- Programmatic guaranteed
- A preferred deal
- The open exchange
answer: 1
explain: Programmatic guaranteed locks both volume and price and sits at the top of the priority stack. A preferred deal fixes price with no volume commitment, and a private marketplace is an invite-only auction above a floor.
:::

:::q Deals & Pricing
question: A client buys at a net CPM of 8.50 dollars after a 15 percent agency commission. To recover the gross CPM the buyer actually pays, you should:
- Multiply 8.50 by 1.15
- Divide 8.50 by 0.85
- Subtract 15 percent from 8.50
- Leave it at 8.50, since net and gross are the same
answer: 1
explain: Net equals gross times one minus the fee, so to gross up you divide by one minus the fee: 8.50 divided by 0.85 is 10 dollars. Adding 15 percent back understates the gross because the fee is a share of the larger gross number.
:::

:::q Yield & Inventory
question: A publisher's ad server returns a paid ad on 850 of 1,000 ad requests. What is the fill rate, and what happened to the rest?
- 85 percent fill, and the 150 unfilled requests earned nothing and are gone forever
- 85 percent fill, and the 150 unfilled requests roll over to the next day
- 15 percent fill, and the rest were sold at a discount
- 100 percent fill, since every request eventually clears
answer: 0
explain: Fill rate is paid impressions divided by requests, here 850 over 1,000, or 85 percent. Inventory is perishable, so the 150 unfilled requests expired the moment the page loaded and earned nothing.

:::q Yield & Inventory
question: Why can setting a price floor too high actually reduce a publisher's total revenue?
- High floors raise the agency commission the publisher owes
- Bids fall below the floor, the impression goes unfilled, and that revenue is lost forever
- Floors only apply to direct-sold inventory, so open-market revenue is unchanged
- A high floor lowers the click-through rate, which scares buyers away
answer: 1
explain: An impression is perishable. If every bid lands below the floor, the impression clears at nothing and that revenue is gone, so floors must be optimized for price times fill, not for the highest CPM alone.
:::

- About 72 percent

- 85 percent
- 30 percent

:::q Margin & Profitability
question: A book of business reports a healthy blended margin, yet several accounts are losing money. What does this illustrate about blended margin?
- Blended margin is exact and cannot hide unprofitable accounts
- Blended margin is an average, so a few high-margin accounts can mask money-losing ones
- Blended margin only counts self-serve fees, not managed-service resale
- A healthy blend guarantees every account is profitable
answer: 1
explain: Blended margin is an average across deal types, and averages conceal. Strong accounts can mask unprofitable ones, and the blend can erode quietly as the mix shifts, so you monitor the components, not just the headline number.

:::q Deals & Pricing
question: A publisher sells 2,000,000 ad opportunities in a day for 9,000 dollars in total revenue. What effective CPM did the sell side realize across those opportunities?
- 2 dollars
- 3.50 dollars
- 4.50 dollars
- 9 dollars
answer: 2
explain: Effective CPM is total revenue divided by impressions, times 1,000: 9,000 divided by 2,000,000 times 1,000 is 4.50 dollars. Leaving opportunities unsold would pull this realized number down, which is why the sell side packages inventory into deals.

:::q Deals & Pricing
question: A buyer pays a 1.50 dollar CPC on inventory running a 0.2 percent click-through rate. What is the equivalent CPM?
- 0.30 dollars
- 3 dollars
- 7.50 dollars
- 30 dollars
answer: 1
explain: CPM equals CTR times 1,000 times CPC: 0.002 times 1,000 times 1.50 is 3 dollars. A thousand impressions at a 0.2 percent CTR produce 2 clicks, so 2 times 1.50 dollars is 3 dollars of cost per thousand impressions.
:::

:::q Deals & Pricing
question: A performance buyer pays a 3 dollar CPC and converts clicks at a 4 percent conversion rate. What is the effective CPA?
- 12 dollars
- 0.12 dollars
- 60 dollars
- 75 dollars
answer: 3
explain: CPA equals CPC divided by the conversion rate: 3 divided by 0.04 is 75 dollars. It takes 25 clicks to land one conversion at a 4 percent CVR, so 25 times 3 dollars is 75 dollars. Whoever prices on the deepest funnel metric absorbs the most variance if the rate slips.
:::

- 54,000 dollars

- 166,667 dollars
- 540,000 dollars
answer: 0
explain: Revenue equals impressions divided by 1,000, times the CPM: 3,000,000 divided by 1,000 is 3,000, times 18 dollars is 54,000 dollars. Because both volume and price are fixed in PG, this revenue is guaranteed as long as the publisher delivers, so the publisher carries the delivery risk.

:::q Yield & Inventory
question: Raw supply for a targeting is 6,000,000 impressions over the flight. 1,500,000 are already committed to other guaranteed deals, and you expect to fill 80 percent of what remains. How many impressions can you safely promise this buyer?
- 4,500,000 impressions
- 4,800,000 impressions
- 3,600,000 impressions
- 6,000,000 impressions
answer: 2
explain: Net out commitments first, then apply fill: (6,000,000 minus 1,500,000) times 0.80 is 4,500,000 times 0.80, which is 3,600,000 impressions. Applying fill before subtracting commitments, or skipping the netting, would over-promise and risk under-delivery and makegoods.
:::

:::q Yield & Inventory
question: Floor A clears a 5 dollar eCPM at a 90 percent fill rate. Floor B is raised to a 6 dollar eCPM but fill drops to 70 percent. Which floor maximizes total yield per thousand requests?
- Floor B, because its higher CPM always wins
- Floor A, because 4.50 dollars beats floor B's 4.20 dollars
- They tie, since price times fill is constant
- Neither, because fill rate does not affect yield
answer: 1
explain: Total yield per thousand requests is eCPM times fill rate. Floor A yields 5 times 0.90, which is 4.50 dollars, while floor B yields 6 times 0.70, which is 4.20 dollars. The higher floor lifts CPM but loses enough fill to lower total yield, so the yield-maximizing floor is A, not the highest CPM in isolation.
:::

- 65,000 dollars

- 68,850 dollars

:::q Margin & Profitability
question: A book has 600,000 dollars of revenue at a 50 percent margin and 400,000 dollars of revenue at a 10 percent margin. What is the blended margin across the whole book?
- 34 percent
- 30 percent
- 40 percent
- 50 percent
answer: 0
explain: Blended margin is total gross profit divided by total revenue. Profit is 600,000 times 0.50 plus 400,000 times 0.10, which is 300,000 plus 40,000, or 340,000 dollars. Blended margin is 340,000 divided by 1,000,000 times 100, which is 34 percent. The thin-margin half drags the blend well below the headline 50 percent.
:::

:::q Margin & Profitability
question: A managed-service account bills 50,000 dollars in media, but the team's service labor to run it costs 20,000 dollars. What is the margin on revenue for this account?
- 40 percent
- 60 percent
- 150 percent
- 30 percent
answer: 1
explain: Margin on revenue is revenue minus cost divided by revenue: (50,000 minus 20,000) divided by 50,000, which is 30,000 divided by 50,000, or 60 percent. Managed service earns a high margin per account, but that 20,000 dollars of labor does not shrink as you add accounts, so the model does not scale without headcount.

:::q ROI & Revenue
