# From co-pilot to autonomous agent

The first wave of AI in buying was a co-pilot that suggested and a human that approved. The next wave is agentic: a system that takes a goal plus a set of guardrails and then plans, transacts, and optimizes end to end, on its own, inside those limits. The agent does not just recommend a bid. It runs the campaign.

PubMatic introduced AgenticOS at CES in January 2026, described as more than twenty agents working together across the buying workflow. The Trade Desk's Koa added Adaptive Trading Modes, including a Performance Mode that moves the system from advisor toward operator. The pattern is the same as the broader platform shift, but with more autonomy: where Module 1's pattern handed bidding to AI, agentic buying hands it the whole plan-bid-optimize loop.

These systems are still mostly human-supervised. That supervision is the point of this lesson.

# The validated case: Telefonica Movistar

Most agentic numbers you will see are vendor claims. One case has been reported with enough specificity to teach from, while still being a reported result rather than an independent audit. PubMatic worked with Havas on a campaign for Telefonica's Movistar brand. The reported outcomes: a CPM that came in 18% below target, impressions delivered 23% above plan, and campaign setup time cut by up to 80%.

Read those three numbers as the shape of the agentic value proposition. Two of them, cheaper media and more of it, are about efficiency at auction time. The third, setup time, is about the human hours the agent removes from planning and trafficking. That third number is the one that changes your job.

:::predict
prompt: A media plan calls for 10,000,000 impressions. The Telefonica Movistar agentic case reported delivering impressions 23% above plan. How many impressions does 23% above plan represent?
answer: 12300000
tolerance: 1000
unit: impressions
hint: 23% above plan means 123% of the planned number. Multiply 10,000,000 by 1.23.
explain: 23% above plan means 100% plus 23%, or 123%, of the plan. 10,000,000 x 1.23 = 12,300,000 impressions. This is the reported magnitude from the PubMatic and Havas case, not an independent benchmark.
:::

:::predict
prompt: A planning and trafficking task normally takes a trader 10 hours. The same case reported setup time cut by up to 80%. At the full 80% reduction, how many hours does the task now take?
answer: 2
tolerance: 0.1
unit: hours
hint: An 80% cut leaves 20% of the original time. Multiply 10 hours by 0.20.
explain: An 80% reduction leaves 100% minus 80%, or 20%, of the time. 10 x 0.20 = 2 hours. The reclaimed 8 hours is exactly the time you should redirect from button-pushing toward writing better objectives and auditing the agent.
:::

# The new job: writing objectives and guardrails

When the agent runs the loop, the leverage moves to the inputs you give it. A good objective is specific and measurable: a target CPA, a target ROAS, a frequency cap, a brand-safety floor, a maximum CPM, an allowed inventory list. A vague objective produces a confidently wrong campaign, because the agent will optimize exactly what you said, not what you meant.

Guardrails are the constraints the agent may never cross even while chasing the objective. Think of the optimization as maximizing expected value subject to limits you declare:

$$ \max\ E[value]\quad \text{subject to}\quad CPA \le CPA_{max},\ freq \le f_{max},\ inventory \in allowlist $$

The skill is no longer nudging bids. It is specifying this problem well. A frequency cap that is too loose burns budget on the same user; a max CPA set below what the value math can support starves the campaign of delivery. The earlier identity, bid value equals pCTR times pCVR times value per conversion, is what tells you whether your guardrails are even feasible.

:::callout warning
An agent optimizes the objective you wrote, not the one you intended. It can also hallucinate targeting: invent audience segments or inventory rationales that sound plausible but do not correspond to anything real. Never accept an agent's plan on its confidence alone. Require it to show the segments, the inventory, and the bid logic, and check that each one actually exists and matches your guardrails.
:::

:::quiz
question: An agentic system is given the single objective "maximize conversions" with no guardrails. What is the most likely failure?
- It chases conversions at any cost, blowing past a sustainable CPA and buying low-quality inventory
- It refuses to spend any budget
- It automatically infers your margin and protects profitability
- It will always match a human trader's results exactly
hint: An agent optimizes exactly what you declared and nothing you left out.
answer: 0
explain: With no cost or quality constraint, "maximize conversions" tells the agent to buy conversions at any price. It will overspend per conversion and accept poor inventory because nothing in the objective penalizes either. Guardrails like a max CPA and an inventory allowlist are what keep an autonomous agent inside the box.
:::

# Auditing instead of optimizing

Once the agent runs the loop, your day-to-day work is auditing, not optimizing. Auditing means asking the system to explain its decisions, checking those explanations against independent evidence, and confirming the agent stayed inside its guardrails.

A practical audit loop: demand decision logs, verify a sample of placements against your allowlist, compare the agent's reported performance against a holdout or third-party measurement, and confirm no guardrail was silently relaxed. If the agent cannot produce a log or a justification for a decision, treat that as a red flag, not a minor gap. The transparency you can extract is the only thing standing between you and a black box spending your budget.
