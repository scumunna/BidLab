# Bidding: pricing each impression, then shading

Every automated bid starts from the expected value of the impression. The model predicts a click-through rate and a conversion rate, multiplies them by what a conversion is worth, and gets a value:

$$ bid\ value = pCTR \times pCVR \times value\ per\ conversion $$

In a first-price auction you pay exactly what you bid, so bidding the full value leaves money on the table. The model instead bids the minimum it thinks will still win. The gap between the value and the actual bid is the bid shade:

$$ bid\ shade = predicted\ value - actual\ bid $$

Reinforcement learning makes this gap adaptive. Rather than one fixed shading rule, an RL agent learns per impression how aggressively to shade from your own win and conversion data, treating each auction as a step that pays off in wins and conversions. Recent work, including "Generative Bid Shading" in 2025, frames the shade itself as a learned quantity. The figures sometimes quoted for RL pacing, such as large percentage lifts in conversions, are secondary and should be read as claimed.

:::figure rtbFlow
caption: In a first-price real-time auction, the buyer's model prices the impression, shades the bid down toward the minimum likely to win, and returns it in milliseconds. The shade is the gap reinforcement learning is trained to set.
:::

:::predict
prompt: A model values an impression at $0.40 and, in a first-price auction, shades its bid down to $0.32. What is the bid shade in dollars?
answer: 0.08
tolerance: 0.005
unit: $
hint: The shade is predicted value minus the actual bid.
explain: bid shade = predicted value - actual bid = $0.40 - $0.32 = $0.08. In a first-price auction this $0.08 is the saving per won impression versus bidding full value, and it is exactly the quantity reinforcement learning is trained to optimize.
:::

:::predict
prompt: Using the same impression valued at $0.40 with a bid of $0.32, what is the bid shade expressed as a percentage of the predicted value?
answer: 20
tolerance: 0.5
unit: %
hint: Divide the shade by the predicted value, then multiply by 100.
explain: The shade is $0.40 - $0.32 = $0.08. As a percent of value: $0.08 / $0.40 x 100 = 20%. Shading 20% means the model bid 80% of value and bet that was still enough to win.
:::

# Creative: dynamic creative optimization as a bandit

Dynamic creative optimization, or DCO, turns one ad into a combinatorial space. Swap two headlines, three images, and two calls to action and you have twelve variants. Generative AI now produces these variants and, in CTV DCO, can assemble video variations in real time. The question is not how to make them but how to find the winner without wasting impressions on losers.

That is exactly a multi-armed bandit problem. Each variant is an arm with an unknown true performance. You must balance exploration, trying each arm enough to learn its rate, against exploitation, sending traffic to the arm that looks best so far. A pure A/B split explores forever; a bandit shifts impressions toward winners as confidence grows, which is why it finds the winning variant faster and refreshes fatigued creative with less waste. Any "higher ROAS versus static" figures from vendors are claimed, not independent.

:::figure videoAd
caption: A CTV video ad assembled by dynamic creative optimization. Each combination of opening shot, voiceover, and end card is one arm of a multi-armed bandit; the system allocates impressions to learn which combination converts best while minimizing spend on the losers.
:::

:::predict
prompt: A static creative converts at a 0.024 click-through rate. A DCO bandit finds a winning variant that converts at 0.030. What is the relative lift of the winning variant over the static creative, as a percentage?
answer: 25
tolerance: 0.5
unit: %
hint: Relative lift is the difference divided by the baseline, times 100.
explain: Relative lift = (0.030 - 0.024) / 0.024 x 100 = 0.006 / 0.024 x 100 = 25%. The value of the bandit is reaching this winning arm quickly while spending as few impressions as possible on the weaker variants.
:::

:::quiz
question: Why is dynamic creative optimization framed as a multi-armed bandit rather than a fixed A/B test?
- A bandit shifts impressions toward better-performing variants as confidence grows, reducing waste on losers while still exploring
- A bandit never tests more than one variant
- A bandit ignores performance data entirely
- An A/B test always finds the winner faster than a bandit
hint: Think about the tradeoff between exploring all variants and exploiting the current best.
answer: 0
explain: A fixed A/B split keeps sending equal traffic to every variant even after a loser is obvious, wasting impressions. A bandit balances exploration and exploitation, steadily moving traffic to stronger arms while still sampling the rest, so it converges on the winner with less waste.
:::

# Measurement: Bayesian marketing-mix models

When cookies and deterministic tracking erode, attribution gives way to marketing-mix modeling. Google open-sourced Meridian in February 2025 and Meta maintains Robyn; both are Bayesian MMMs that rebuild measurement for a post-cookie world. An MMM decomposes total sales into the contribution of each channel, then layers in two effects that linear thinking misses.

Adstock captures that advertising carries over: a flight today still drives sales next week. Diminishing returns, or saturation, captures that the tenth million in spend buys less lift than the first. A simplified contribution for a channel looks like a saturating function of adstocked spend:

$$ contribution = \beta \times f\big(adstock(spend)\big) $$

The Bayesian part estimates the coefficient beta and the curve shape with uncertainty, as distributions rather than single points. But a coefficient alone only means correlated. To make it mean caused, you calibrate the model against incrementality experiments, geo holdouts or lift tests, so the fitted curves are anchored to measured causal lift. That calibration is what separates a credible MMM from a curve-fit.

:::widget adstockExplorer
effect: Increase the adstock decay and watch how a single burst of spend keeps contributing to sales over later periods. This carryover is one of the two effects, alongside saturation, that a Bayesian MMM must capture and that simple last-click attribution misses entirely.
:::

:::widget saturationExplorer
effect: Push spend up a channel's response curve and watch the marginal return flatten. Diminishing returns are why a Bayesian MMM fits a saturating curve, and why the coefficient must be calibrated against incrementality experiments before you read it as caused rather than merely correlated.
:::

:::callout warning
An MMM coefficient is only as causal as its calibration. Uncalibrated, the model can confidently assign credit to a channel that was merely correlated with sales, the same hallucinated-causation trap that black-box automation invites. Always anchor an MMM to geo holdouts or lift tests, and treat any uncalibrated channel coefficient as correlation, not proof of cause.
:::
