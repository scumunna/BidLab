# Features, labels, and the prediction the bid is built on

Every automated bid resolves to a number a model predicted. To audit that model you have to know what it was fed and what it was asked to predict. The inputs are features: signals attached to the bid request such as device, placement, time of day, page context, and audience data. The thing it predicts is the label: did the click happen, did the conversion happen. A model learns the relationship between features and labels from history, then applies it to a fresh impression it has never seen.

In a click model the label is binary, click or no click, and the model outputs a probability between 0 and 1. That probability is the predicted click-through rate, the pCTR, and it is the first term in the value chain from earlier modules:

$$ bid\ value = pCTR \times pCVR \times value\ per\ conversion $$

The AI predicts the two probabilities. You own the value. Knowing that pCTR is a learned function of features, not a fact, is what lets you ask the right question when a campaign misbehaves: is the model wrong, or is the value wrong.

The model outputs a probability for that label, which is the pCTR. The bid price and value are downstream of the prediction, not inputs to it.

# Training versus inference: learn once, predict forever (until you retrain)

A model lives in two distinct modes, and confusing them is a common operational mistake. Training is the offline phase where the model sees historical features paired with their known labels and adjusts its parameters to fit that data. Inference is the online phase where the trained, frozen model takes a live request and outputs a prediction in the milliseconds an auction allows.

The gap between the two is where most production failures hide. The model is trained on the past and serves the present, so when the world shifts, a holiday, a new creative, a seasonal demand spike, the frozen model keeps predicting as if nothing changed until someone retrains it. This is why platforms run a learning period: after a major change, Smart Bidding re-enters an exploration phase, typically one to two weeks, to rebuild its prediction model before performance stabilizes.

:::callout key
Training fits the model on labeled history and happens offline. Inference applies the frozen model to a live request and happens in milliseconds at auction time. A model only knows the world as of its last training run, so the operational risk is staleness: the world moves, the frozen model does not, and the bids drift until you retrain.
:::

:::predict

tolerance: 0.1

explain: An 80% reduction leaves 100% minus 80%, or 20%, of the task. 10 x 0.20 = 2 hours. :::

# The bias-variance tradeoff: underfitting versus overfitting

Every model sits somewhere on a tension between two failure modes, and naming them is half the job of diagnosing a bad campaign. Bias is error from a model too simple to capture the real pattern. A high-bias model underfits: it ignores genuine structure in the data and is wrong in the same direction everywhere. Variance is error from a model too sensitive to the particular training sample. A high-variance model overfits: it memorizes noise, looks excellent on training data, and falls apart on impressions it has not seen.

You cannot drive both to zero at once. Reducing bias usually means adding complexity, which raises variance. Reducing variance usually means simplifying or constraining the model, which raises bias. The goal is the sweet spot that minimizes total error on new data, not on the data you fit. Total expected error is often written as the sum of three parts:

$$ error = bias^2 + variance + noise $$

The noise term is irreducible. The job is to trade the other two against each other until their sum is as small as it can be on data the model has never seen.

- High variance: the model is overfitting, memorizing training noise instead of generalizable pattern

explain: A large gap between strong training performance and weak performance on unseen data is the signature of high variance, or overfitting. The model fit noise specific to the training sample. The fix is to reduce variance: regularize, simplify, or add more diverse training data. High bias would instead show poor performance on both training and fresh data.

# Evaluation: ranking, loss, and calibration are three different questions

You cannot audit a model with one number. Three metrics answer three separate questions, and a model can ace one while failing another.

AUC, the area under the ROC curve, measures ranking: across many randomly drawn pairs of one clicked and one non-clicked impression, AUC is the share of pairs where the model gave the clicked one the higher score. An AUC of 0.5 is random; 1.0 is perfect ordering. AUC tells you whether the model sorts good impressions above bad ones. It says nothing about whether the predicted probabilities are numerically right.

Log loss, the negative log-likelihood, penalizes predictions that are both confident and wrong, and rewards predicted probabilities that converge to the true rate. Lower is better.

Calibration asks the question the bid actually depends on: when the model says 2%, does the event happen about 2% of the time? A standard summary is the calibration ratio, the average predicted rate divided by the true rate, where 1.0 is perfect. Researchers also use expected calibration error, which bins predictions and averages the gap between predicted confidence and observed frequency in each bin.

:::predict
prompt: To estimate AUC you draw 1000 random pairs, each with one clicked and one non-clicked impression. The model gives the clicked impression the higher score in 820 of the pairs. What is the estimated AUC?
answer: 0.82
tolerance: 0.005
unit:
hint: AUC is the fraction of pairs where the clicked impression is ranked above the non-clicked one. Divide 820 by 1000.
explain: AUC is exactly the probability that a randomly chosen clicked impression scores higher than a randomly chosen non-clicked one. 820 / 1000 = 0.82. That is a solid ranking model, but it tells you nothing about calibration: the model could rank perfectly and still systematically predict the wrong probability level, which is the failure the next section is about.
:::

# Why a miscalibrated pCTR overbids your budget

Here is the foundation that ties the math to your money. In a cost-per-click system the effective price the model is willing to pay per thousand impressions is built directly from the predicted click rate:

$$ eCPM = 1000 \times pCTR \times bid_{CPC} $$

If the pCTR is too high, the eCPM is too high by the same proportion, and the system overbids on every impression it touches. A model can have excellent AUC, ranking impressions in exactly the right order, and still be badly miscalibrated, predicting 2.5% where the truth is 2.0%. Ranking is preserved, but every bid is inflated 25%. Researchers have shown this directly: calibration is the average predicted rate over the true rate, and it directly affects the downstream bids in ad auctions and the amount of money charged to advertisers.

This is why calibration, not AUC, is the metric an auditor watches. AUC protects ranking; calibration protects your spend.

:::predict
prompt: A model predicts a click-through rate of 0.025, but the true click-through rate for those impressions is 0.020. What is the calibration ratio, predicted divided by actual?
answer: 1.25
tolerance: 0.01
unit:
hint: Calibration ratio is the average predicted rate divided by the true rate. Divide 0.025 by 0.020.
explain: Calibration ratio = predicted / actual = 0.025 / 0.020 = 1.25. A ratio of 1.25 means the model overpredicts clicks by 25%. Because eCPM is proportional to pCTR, this same 25% flows straight into the bid: the model overbids by 25% on every impression even if its ranking, its AUC, is flawless.
:::

:::predict
prompt: Continuing the case above, the bid CPC is $2.00. Calibrated eCPM uses the true rate 0.020; the miscalibrated eCPM uses the predicted 0.025. How many dollars higher is the miscalibrated eCPM?
answer: 10
tolerance: 0.05
unit: $
hint: eCPM = 1000 x rate x bid CPC. Compute both, then subtract.
explain: Calibrated eCPM = 1000 x 0.020 x $2.00 = $40.00. Miscalibrated eCPM = 1000 x 0.025 x $2.00 = $50.00. The difference is $50.00 - $40.00 = $10.00, exactly 25% over, the same 1.25 calibration ratio expressed in dollars. That $10 per thousand impressions is real budget the miscalibration burns.
:::

# Overfitting in production, and the guardrails that catch it

Overfitting is not a textbook abstraction in adtech; it is a recurring production failure. A model trained too hard on a narrow window memorizes the quirks of that window, a single high-performing placement, a holiday spike, a bot pattern, and then bids confidently on those quirks when they no longer hold. The defenses are standard machine learning hygiene, and an auditor should confirm each is in place.

Regularization, such as Lasso or Ridge penalties, constrains the model so it cannot chase every wiggle in the data, trading a little bias for much less variance. A held-out validation set, data the model never trained on, is the only honest way to estimate real-world error, since training error always flatters an overfit model. And recalibration, refitting the mapping from raw score to probability against recent outcomes, keeps the calibration ratio near 1.0 as the world drifts.

The thread through this whole module: an automated bid is only as trustworthy as the model behind it, and the model is only as trustworthy as its calibration on data it has never seen.

:::callout warning
A model that looks brilliant on training data and fails on fresh impressions is overfit, and an overfit or stale model overbids precisely because it is confident and wrong. Demand three things before trusting an automated bidder: performance measured on a held-out set, not training data; a calibration ratio reported near 1.0, not just a high AUC; and a retraining and recalibration cadence frequent enough to track the market. Confidence is not accuracy, and accuracy is not calibration.
:::
