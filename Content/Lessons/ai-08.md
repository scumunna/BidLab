# Measurement moved back to the model

For a decade the default measurement story was a user-level one: a cookie linked an impression to a click to a conversion, and an attribution rule split the credit. That story broke. Safari and Firefox block third-party cookies, Apple's App Tracking Transparency cut the mobile signal, and even though Chrome kept third-party cookies after shutting down the Privacy Sandbox in October 2025, the deterministic path is too leaky to trust alone. So measurement moved up a level, from tracking individuals to modeling aggregates, and the model doing the work is increasingly automated.

Marketing mix modeling, or MMM, is the method that came back. An MMM regresses total business outcomes (sales, installs, signups) against media spend by channel plus controls like seasonality and price. It never needs a user identifier, which is exactly why it survives privacy loss. The new part is that building one no longer requires a six-figure consulting engagement, because the two largest platforms open-sourced their MMM frameworks.

:::quiz
question: Why did marketing mix modeling regain prominence as cookie-based tracking eroded?
- It models aggregate outcomes against spend and needs no user-level identifier
- It tracks each individual user more precisely than a cookie
- It replaces the need for any experiments or holdouts
- It only works inside a single walled garden
hint: Think about what an MMM needs as input versus what attribution needs.
answer: 0
explain: An MMM regresses aggregate outcomes against channel spend and controls. It never links an impression to a person, so it is unaffected by the loss of third-party cookies and mobile identifiers. That privacy resilience, not better tracking, is why it returned.
:::

# The open-source MMM stack: Robyn and Meridian

Two frameworks anchor the modern stack. Meta Marketing Science maintains Robyn, an AI assisted MMM that uses Nevergrad, Meta's gradient-free optimizer, to search hyperparameters for adstock and saturation and return a set of Pareto-optimal models rather than one hand-tuned fit. Google open-sourced Meridian, a Bayesian MMM, with general availability in early 2025. Meridian uses Bayesian causal inference, geo-level data, and a No-U-Turn sampler, and it is explicitly built to be calibrated with experiments.

Both encode the same two non-linear effects that simple regression misses. Adstock captures carryover: spend today still drives outcomes next week. Saturation captures diminishing returns: the tenth million in spend buys less lift than the first. A channel's contribution is a coefficient times a saturating function of adstocked spend.

$$ contribution = \beta \times f\big(adstock(spend)\big) $$

The automation is real and useful, but it does not make the output causal on its own. A fitted coefficient means correlated until you anchor it to a real experiment.

caption: Saturation in an MMM bends the response curve over, so each additional dollar of adstocked spend returns less outcome than the last. The automated framework fits this curve shape, but the coefficient is only causal once calibrated against an experiment.
:::

# AI-assisted incrementality

An MMM tells you a channel's modeled contribution. Incrementality tells you what a campaign actually caused by comparing a treated group to a control that never saw the ad. Lift is the treatment outcome rate minus the control rate, expressed over the control baseline.

$$ Lift = (CVR_{test} - CVR_{control}) / CVR_{control} $$

AI changed the operations of incrementality more than the math. Geo experiments that once took a data science team to design are now packaged. Open-source tooling like Meta's GeoLift, and commercial platforms built on the same econometric methods, let a marketer design and read a geo test without a bespoke build. The synthetic control method these tools use constructs a weighted blend of untreated regions that matches the treated region's pre-period trend, which is a model doing the matching for you.

The reason incrementality matters here is calibration. A well-run experiment is treated as ground truth, and the MMM is fit to honor that measured lift rather than free-fitting every coefficient. That is what turns a curve-fit into a credible causal estimate.

:::predict
prompt: A geo holdout measures a treatment conversion rate of 4.5% and a control conversion rate of 3.6%. What is the incremental lift over baseline, as a percent?
answer: 25
tolerance: 0.2
unit: %
hint: Lift is (test minus control) divided by control, times 100.
explain: Lift = (4.5% - 3.6%) / 3.6% x 100 = 0.9 / 3.6 x 100 = 25%. The campaign lifted conversions 25% above the baseline that would have converted with no exposure, and this measured lift is the anchor an MMM should be calibrated to.
:::

:::callout key
An MMM coefficient is only as causal as its calibration. Use AI-assisted geo experiments to produce ground-truth lift, then calibrate the model to it. An uncalibrated channel coefficient is correlation, not proof of cause.
:::

# Attention as a measured input

Viewability told you an ad could be seen. Attention measurement asks whether anyone actually looked. The metric that became the industry shorthand is Adelaide's Attention Unit, or AU, a 0 to 100 score that blends viewability, time in view, ad size, clutter, and contextual signals into one normalized number that predicts outcomes. In October 2025 Nielsen and Adelaide announced a collaboration that puts AU inside Nielsen ONE, pairing reach with a quality-of-reach score, and the IAB and MRC moved to standardize how attention is measured across vendors.

For a measurement job, attention is a feature, not a verdict. A higher AU placement should correlate with more lift, but it is still a model's prediction. Treat AU the way you treat a pCTR: a useful input you validate against outcomes, not an outcome itself.

:::quiz
question: What is the Attention Unit (AU) metric most accurately described as?

- A direct count of completed purchases from an ad
- A guarantee that 100 percent of an ad was on screen
- A replacement for any outcome measurement
hint: It is a predictive score built from viewability, time in view, and context.
answer: 0
explain: AU is a normalized score (roughly 0 to 100) that synthesizes viewability, time in view, format, clutter, and context to predict how much attention a placement earns and how likely that attention drives outcomes. It is a predictive input, not a count of conversions.
:::

# The black-box risk, and why you triangulate

An automated MMM can overfit. With many channels, flexible adstock and saturation shapes, and a short history, a model can fit the noise in last year's sales and report confident contributions that do not hold out of sample. Bayesian frameworks help by carrying uncertainty as distributions rather than single points, and hyperparameter search returns multiple candidate models, but neither removes the danger of a plausible, well-fit, wrong answer that you cannot easily inspect.

The discipline that contains this is triangulation: never trust one method alone. MMM gives strategic breadth across all spend, incrementality gives causal proof on the channels you can test, and attribution gives tactical day-to-day granularity. When all three point the same way you act with confidence. When they diverge, the divergence is the finding, and you investigate before you reallocate. A single black-box number that grades its own homework is exactly the input you should distrust most.

:::callout warning
Automated MMM can overfit and present a confident, opaque, wrong answer. Defend with triangulation: reconcile MMM against incrementality experiments and attribution. Convergence builds trust, divergence is a signal to dig, and no single self-graded model gets the final word.
:::

:::quiz
