# Computing on data you never get to hold

Privacy-safe AI in advertising solves one problem: you want to train models and measure outcomes across two parties' data without either party handing over the raw records. The cookie made this casual, because identifiers flowed freely. With identifiers restricted, the new architectures move the computation to the data instead of moving the data to the computation.

There are two broad shapes. In a data clean room, both parties' data lands in a neutral, governed environment where only approved aggregate queries can run, and raw rows never leave. In federated and on-device learning, the data never moves at all: the model goes to the device or the silo, trains locally, and only model updates or aggregates come back. Both replace the old assumption that to use data you must first collect it.

:::

# Data clean rooms

A clean room is a controlled environment where an advertiser and a partner, often a walled-garden platform, each bring data, match it on hashed identifiers, and run only pre-approved analyses that return aggregates above a minimum count. The advertiser never sees the partner's user rows and the partner never sees the advertiser's. Amazon Marketing Cloud running on AWS Clean Rooms is a live example: advertisers match first-party signals against Amazon's signals without moving the underlying data out of their own AWS environment.

The hard number in a clean room is the match rate, the share of your records that successfully join to the partner's. It caps how much of your audience the analysis can even see, and it is never 100 percent because of hashing differences, stale records, and users present on one side only. By 2025 the standalone clean-room category had matured and partly folded into the cloud platforms, but the matching mechanic is the same wherever it runs.

:::predict
prompt: An advertiser uploads 5,000,000 hashed email records into a clean room. 3,200,000 of them match a record on the partner's side. What is the match rate?
answer: 64
tolerance: 0.5
unit: %
hint: Match rate is matched records divided by total records uploaded.
explain: 3,200,000 / 5,000,000 = 0.64, a 64% match rate. The unmatched 36% are invisible to any analysis in that clean room, so a low match rate quietly shrinks the population every downstream aggregate is computed over.
:::

# Differential privacy and the epsilon budget

Matching and thresholds are not enough on their own, because repeated aggregate queries can still leak an individual through difference attacks. Differential privacy closes that gap by adding calibrated random noise to each released result, so that whether any single person is in the data or not, the published answer is statistically almost the same.

The control knob is epsilon. A smaller epsilon means more noise, stronger privacy, and noisier results. A larger epsilon means less noise, weaker privacy, and sharper results. The IAB Tech Lab's differential privacy guidance frames epsilon as a privacy budget that you spend. Because privacy loss accumulates across releases (composition), a privacy accountant tracks the total, and under simple sequential composition the budget splits across the reports you publish.

$$ \epsilon_{per\ report} = \epsilon_{total} / n_{reports} $$

:::predict
prompt: You set a total privacy budget of epsilon = 2.0 for a campaign and plan to publish 5 weekly aggregate reports, splitting the budget equally under sequential composition. What epsilon does each weekly report get?
answer: 0.4
tolerance: 0.01
unit:
hint: Divide the total budget by the number of reports.
explain: Under sequential composition the spent epsilons add up, so an equal split is total divided by count: 2.0 / 5 = 0.4 per report. Publish more often from the same total budget and each report gets a smaller epsilon, meaning more noise and a fuzzier number.
:::

# Federated learning and on-device modeling

Federated learning takes privacy-safe computation further: the raw data never leaves the device or the silo. A shared model is sent out, each device trains on its own local data, and only the model updates (gradients) come back to be averaged into a new global model. On-device modeling is the inference side of the same idea, where the model runs locally so the signal never has to be transmitted.

This was the bet behind much of Google's Privacy Sandbox: Topics, Protected Audience, and Attribution Reporting tried to move interest grouping and bid logic into the browser. That bet did not pay off. Google retired most Privacy Sandbox APIs in October 2025 after low adoption and regulatory pressure, and kept third-party cookies in Chrome. The lesson for a practitioner is to separate the technique from the product. Federated and on-device learning are sound and still used (Apple and Google use them for keyboard and analytics models), but a specific shipped API can fail in market while the underlying idea remains valid.

# Aggregated measurement and the privacy-utility tradeoff

Tie the pieces together with aggregated measurement: instead of a per-user conversion log, you receive counts and sums for cohorts, protected by thresholds and noise. Conversions by channel, reach, and trendlines survive this well because they are aggregates. One-to-one retargeting and precise per-user attribution do not, because that is exactly the individual-level signal these systems are designed to withhold.

Every one of these systems is tuning the same dial: the privacy-utility tradeoff. Stronger privacy (smaller epsilon, higher count thresholds, more aggregation) means noisier and coarser numbers. Weaker privacy means sharper numbers and more re-identification risk. There is no setting that maximizes both, so the operational skill is choosing a point on the curve that is private enough to be permitted and useful enough to act on, and knowing which questions a privacy-safe pipeline simply cannot answer.

:::callout key
Every privacy-safe method tunes one dial: privacy versus utility. Smaller epsilon, higher thresholds, and more aggregation buy privacy at the cost of precision. Pick the point on the curve that is compliant and still actionable, and accept that user-level questions are off the table by design.
:::
