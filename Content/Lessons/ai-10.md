# Governance is now part of the buy

For most of the last decade, handing optimization to a model was a performance question. In 2026 it is also a compliance question. If you run AI buying, AI creative, or AI audience tooling for campaigns that touch the European Union, or for advertisers regulated in employment, housing, credit, or insurance, a body of law now sits on top of your media plan. The job is no longer just to make the model perform. It is to be able to show, on demand, what the model is, what it decided, and how you disclosed it.

The center of this is the EU AI Act, the first broad, horizontal AI law from a major economy. It entered into force on August 1, 2024, and its obligations switch on in waves rather than all at once. Around it sits a softer layer of industry self-regulation, the IAB AI Transparency and Disclosure Framework released in January 2026, and underneath both is a fragmented set of US state laws that often disagree with each other.

A governance process is what ties these together so that a single campaign does not need a lawyer per line item. You classify the use, you apply the matching duty, and you keep a record.

# The four risk tiers

The EU AI Act sorts every system into one of four tiers by the risk it poses, and the tier decides how heavy the obligations are.

Unacceptable risk systems are banned outright. These include social scoring by public authorities, certain manipulative systems that exploit vulnerabilities, and most untargeted scraping of facial images. High risk systems are allowed but carry the heaviest duties: risk management, data governance, human oversight, logging, and conformity assessment. This tier covers AI used in areas like employment, credit scoring, and access to essential services. Limited risk systems carry transparency duties only, which is the tier that catches most advertising use of generative AI: you must tell people when they are talking to a bot or looking at synthetic media. Minimal risk systems, which covers the large majority of ordinary AI, carry no new obligations.

For a buyer, the practical takeaway is that classification comes first. A chatbot on a landing page or an AI-generated video ad usually lands in the limited risk tier and owes disclosure. An AI tool that scores job applicants or decides who sees a credit offer can land in high risk and owes far more.

# The phased timeline

The Act does not land all at once. The dates matter because a duty that is not yet in force is not yet enforceable, and a duty that is in force can carry real fines.

The sequence as enacted: entry into force on August 1, 2024. Prohibitions on unacceptable risk systems applied from February 2, 2025. Obligations for general purpose AI models applied from August 2, 2025. The bulk of high risk obligations under Annex III were set for August 2, 2026, and high risk AI embedded in already regulated products under Annex I for August 2, 2027. The transparency duties of Article 50, the ones most advertising touches, apply from August 2, 2026.

One important caveat for 2026. The European Commission proposed a Digital Omnibus package on November 19, 2025 to simplify and stagger parts of the Act, and EU lawmakers reached political agreement on revisions on May 7, 2026. Under that agreement the use based high risk obligations under Annex III are deferred from August 2, 2026 to December 2, 2027, and generative AI models on the market before August 2, 2026 get until December 2, 2026 to apply machine readable marking. Treat these deferrals as agreed in principle but moving through formal adoption, so confirm the live date before you rely on it.

# Transparency duties and disclosure of AI creative

Article 50 is the part of the Act that advertising feels first. It creates four transparency situations. People must be told when they are interacting with an AI system such as a chatbot. Synthetic image, audio, video, and text outputs must be marked in a machine readable format and detectable as AI generated. Deployers must label deepfakes, AI generated or manipulated media that resembles real people or events and could appear authentic. And AI generated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest must be disclosed.

The machine readable side leans on provenance standards. The most common is C2PA, an open standard that embeds a tamper evident record of how a piece of content was made directly into the file. A standardized EU label is being defined through a Code of Practice, with the European Commission publishing draft guidance and a second draft of the transparency Code in early 2026 and a final version expected around mid 2026.

The honest distinction to teach a trading team: not every AI touch needs a consumer facing label, but synthetic media that could mislead does, and the machine readable mark should travel with the asset either way.

:::callout key
Disclosure under Article 50 has two layers. A consumer facing cue, a visible label or badge, is required when synthetic content could mislead. A machine readable mark, typically C2PA, should be attached to AI generated assets so the content can be detected as synthetic downstream even when no visible label is shown.
:::

- A model that picks which inventory to bid on inside a DSP
- An AI tool that resizes and reformats an existing photo for placements

- An internal AI that forecasts pacing for the trader
answer: 2

explain: A deepfake of a real person is the textbook Article 50 case: it resembles an actual individual, could appear authentic, and must be labeled so viewers are not deceived. Bidding models, routine reformatting, and internal forecasting are background uses that do not, by themselves, owe a consumer facing label.
:::

# The IAB framework and US state approaches

Self-regulation moves faster than law, and the industry shipped its own answer. The IAB released its AI Transparency and Disclosure Framework on January 15, 2026, the first of its kind for advertising. It is deliberately risk based and materiality driven rather than blanket labeling: disclosure is required only when AI materially affects authenticity, identity, or representation in ways that could mislead, such as synthetic humans, AI generated voices of real or deceased people, or chatbots posing as human. Routine optimization, copywriting help, and clearly stylized creative do not need a label. It pairs consumer facing cues with C2PA machine readable metadata, the same two layer shape as the law.

The framework is anchored in a trust gap it documents. The IAB found that 82% of advertising executives believed Gen Z and Millennial consumers feel positively about AI generated ads, while only 45% of those consumers actually did. It also found 73% of younger consumers said clear disclosure would increase or maintain their likelihood to purchase, which reframes disclosure as a trust lever, not just a cost.

The US picture is a patchwork. There is no single federal AI law, so state rules govern. Colorado passed the first broad state AI act (SB 24-205) targeting algorithmic discrimination in consequential decisions, but it was repeatedly delayed and then substantially rewritten by an amendment the governor signed on May 14, 2026, shifting to an automated decision making framework effective January 1, 2027. Texas enacted its Responsible AI Governance Act (TRAIGA), which took effect January 1, 2026, focusing on government use and an intent based standard with categorical bans on manipulation and unlawful discrimination. The lesson for a national advertiser is that you cannot assume one rule, you classify per jurisdiction.

# Building an AI governance process

A governance process is the operational machine that turns these rules into repeatable steps. Five parts make it run.

First, an inventory: a living list of every AI system in your stack, who owns it, and what it does. You cannot govern what you have not catalogued. Second, classification: map each use to a risk tier and a jurisdiction, because the tier and the location decide the duty. Third, disclosure rules: a decision tree that outputs either no label, a consumer facing label, a machine readable C2PA mark, or both. Fourth, records and logging: keep the inputs, the model version, and the decision trail so you can answer a regulator or an advertiser without reconstructing it from memory. Fifth, human oversight: a named person who can review, override, and halt an automated system, which the high risk tier requires and which is good practice everywhere.

The fine math is the reason this is not optional. The Act sets penalties up to 35 million euros or 7% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher, for the most serious violations such as prohibited practices. For a large company, the percentage usually dominates the flat figure.

:::predict
prompt: A company has worldwide annual turnover of 800 million euros and faces the top EU AI Act penalty tier of the higher of 35 million euros or 7% of turnover. What is 7% of turnover, in millions of euros?
answer: 56
tolerance: 0.5
unit:
hint: Multiply 800 by 0.07.
explain: 7% of 800 million euros is 800 x 0.07 = 56 million euros. Because 56 million is higher than the 35 million flat cap, the percentage governs, so the maximum exposure here is 56 million euros. For a smaller firm where 7% falls below 35 million, the flat figure would govern instead. That whichever is higher rule is what makes the percentage bite for large advertisers and platforms.
:::
