# A discount is a margin decision

When a buyer asks for 10 percent off, they are asking you to give up margin, not revenue. Those are different sizes. On a deal with a 40 percent margin, a 10 percent price cut does not shave 10 percent off profit. It shaves a quarter of it.

The reason is that discounts come straight off the top line, but your costs do not move. The whole discount is subtracted from the margin, which is the thin slice that was left after costs. Small price cuts hit profit hard.

:::predict
prompt: A deal sells for $100 with $60 of cost, leaving $40 of profit. The buyer wins a 10 percent price cut and cost holds firm. By what percent does the gross profit fall?
answer: 25
unit: %
hint: The whole $10 discount comes out of the $40 profit. Compute the drop as a share of the original profit.
explain: A 10 percent cut on $100 is $10, taken entirely from the $40 profit, leaving $30. The profit falls $10 / $40 = 25%. A 10 percent price cut shaves a quarter of the profit because cost does not move.
:::

# The margin after a discount

If you hold cost fixed and cut price, the new margin percentage follows a simple rule. With margin and discount both as decimals, the post-discount margin is the old margin minus the discount, divided by one minus the discount.

$$ margin_{new} = (margin_{old} - discount) / (1 - discount) $$

A 40 percent margin product discounted 10 percent drops to a 33.3 percent margin. To earn back the lost profit at the new margin, you would need to sell about 33 percent more volume. A 20 percent discount on the same product roughly halves margin, demanding double the volume to break even.

:::predict
prompt: A product carries a 30 percent margin. The buyer wants a 15 percent discount with cost held constant. What is the new margin percentage?
answer: 17.6
unit: %
tolerance: 0.2
hint: Use new margin = (old margin minus discount) divided by (1 minus discount), with both as decimals.
explain: New margin = (0.30 - 0.15) / (1 - 0.15) = 0.15 / 0.85 = 0.1765, about 17.6%. The 15-point price cut comes off the margin and is then spread over a smaller price base, so the margin more than halves.
:::

:::callout warning
Never discount to win a deal without computing the breakeven volume. If a 15 percent cut needs 50 percent more units to hold profit flat, and the buyer is not committing to 50 percent more, you just made the account less profitable.
:::

# Tiers and make-goods

Volume tiers are how you discount without bleeding. Instead of one flat cut, you offer small, growing discounts that unlock only at committed spend thresholds. Each tier should still clear your minimum acceptable margin, so the buyer's savings rise with their commitment and your margin stays predictable.

A make-good is the other side of negotiation: free or bonus delivery you owe when a campaign underdelivers its guaranteed impressions. Price it like a discount, because that is what it is. Bonus impressions carry your full delivery cost but earn zero incremental revenue, so a 5 percent make-good on a thin-margin deal can erase the profit entirely.

:::callout key
Trade discounts for commitments, never for goodwill. A discount tied to a higher spend tier or a longer term protects margin. A discount given to close faster just resets the buyer's expectation for next quarter.
:::
