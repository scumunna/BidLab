# Growth hides in the book you already have

New logos get the attention, but the cheapest revenue you will ever close is more revenue from a customer who already trusts you. The metric that captures this is net revenue retention, and for any recurring or renewing book it is the single best measure of health.

Net revenue retention (NRR) asks one question: if you closed your sales team for a year and signed no new accounts, would your revenue grow or shrink? Above 100 percent and the book grows on its own. Below, and you are running to stand still.

:::predict
prompt: A book worth $5,000,000 in recurring revenue runs a 120 percent NRR. If no new accounts are signed, what is the book worth a year later?
answer: 6000000
unit: $
hint: Apply the NRR as a multiplier to the starting recurring revenue.
explain: Ending value = $5,000,000 x 1.20 = $6,000,000. An NRR above 100 percent means expansion outpaces churn, so the same accounts grow the book by $1,000,000 with no new logos closed.
:::

# The net revenue retention formula

Start with the recurring revenue from a cohort at the beginning of a period. Add expansion (upsells, cross-sells, added seats). Subtract contraction (downgrades) and churn (cancellations). Divide by the starting figure.

$$ NRR = (start + expansion - contraction - churn) / start $$

Three forces move it. Expansion pushes up, contraction and churn pull down. NRR can exceed 100 percent only when expansion more than offsets the losses, which is why expansion selling is the lever, not just churn prevention.

:::predict
prompt: A cohort starts at $800,000 in recurring revenue. It adds $160,000 in expansion, loses $40,000 to downgrades, and $80,000 to cancellations. What is the NRR?
answer: 105
unit: %
hint: NRR is (start plus expansion minus contraction minus churn) divided by start, times 100.
explain: NRR = ($800,000 + $160,000 - $40,000 - $80,000) / $800,000 x 100 = $840,000 / $800,000 x 100 = 105%. Expansion just outran the combined losses, so the book grew 5 percent on its own.
:::

:::callout insight
A 90 percent NRR book loses 10 percent of its value every year before you sell a thing. A 120 percent NRR book gains 20 percent. The same headcount produces wildly different growth depending on which book they are working.
:::

# Why expansion math wins

Expansion revenue carries almost no acquisition cost. You already paid the CAC to land the account, so an upsell converts at a fraction of the cost of a new logo and at a much higher rate. That is why best-in-class SaaS books run NRR above 120 percent, while a healthy median sits near 105 percent and anything under 100 percent signals a leak.

Churn is the silent tax. A 3 percent monthly churn rate compounds to roughly 30 percent of the book lost over a year. Small monthly leaks become large annual holes, so retention work is a numbers game you fight one renewal at a time.

:::callout key
Prioritize accounts by expansion potential, not just size. A mid-size account with room to add seats and products can contribute more NRR than a large account already at full penetration. Map the upside before you build the renewal plan.
:::
