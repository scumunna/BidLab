# Why pharma creative is a regulated asset, not a design choice

In every other vertical the creative is a marketing decision. In pharma it is a regulated promotional statement, and that single fact reshapes the trader's daily job. Any branded ad that makes a product claim must present fair balance, meaning benefit and risk shown together, carrying Important Safety Information (ISI), and every asset must clear Medical, Legal, Regulatory (MLR) review before it serves (21 CFR 202.1, enforced by FDA's Office of Prescription Drug Promotion, OPDP).

The operator does not write the ISI, but the operator owns whether it actually renders, whether automation preserves it, and whether an approved asset is the only thing on the wire. Get that wrong and the failure is not a soft CTR dip, it is a regulatory event that can pause the flight and burn the budget. This lesson is the compliance spine the rest of the path leans on.

:::callout key
Three ad types carry three different ISI regimes. A full product-claim ad needs fair balance and ISI, a reminder ad names the drug with no claim and carries no ISI, and a disease-state ad discusses the condition with no brand and no ISI. Applying one template to all three is a classic MLR miss.
:::

# ISI that actually renders on mobile

The most common in-flight failure is ISI that technically exists but does not render legibly on a phone. The practitioner standard is a hard floor: ISI must never display below 12px regardless of viewport width. A robust CSS pattern is $\text{font-size} = \max(12\text{px},\ 2.5\text{vw})$ inside an `@media (max-width:480px)` block, so the type scales up on larger phones but can never collapse below the floor on a narrow one.

Placement is just as load-bearing as size. The risk information must live in the post itself as scrolling ISI, never gated behind a "see more," a "swipe up," a linked page, or a QR scan. The 2026 OPDP letters reinforce the underlying principle: the recurring risk-presentation findings were disclosures undercut by distracting visuals running during the safety statement and risk copy placed after a transition that signaled the ad had ended, so "in view, never gated, never undercut" is the rule the operator QAs on every placement.

:::predict
prompt: On a 320px-wide phone, the ISI rule sets font-size to max(12px, 2.5vw). What minimum font size in pixels actually renders?
answer: 12
tolerance: 0.1
unit: px
hint: Compute 2.5vw first, then take the larger of that and 12px.
explain: 2.5vw on a 320px viewport is 0.025 times 320 = 8px. The max() takes the larger of 8px and 12px, so 12px renders. The floor is exactly what stops a narrow phone from shrinking ISI below legibility.

# DCO will strip your ISI unless you lock it

Dynamic creative optimization is where compliance quietly breaks. A DCO engine optimizes toward engagement, and an ISI block reads as a low-engagement element, so after roughly 72 hours of A/B testing the algorithm can flag it and remove it from the winning variants. That is not a creative tweak. A served product-claim ad without ISI is a fair-balance violation generated by automation that nobody approved.

The fix is operational, not cosmetic: lock the ISI as non-optimizable so it is carried into every generated variant, commonly by tagging the asset with metadata such as `compliance_required: true`. If a campaign machine-generates 500 variants, the compliant target is not most of them, it is all 500, because a single ISI-stripped variant in rotation is the violation.

:::predict
prompt: A DCO campaign machine-generates 500 product-claim variants. To stay compliant, how many must carry intact ISI?
answer: 500
tolerance: 0
unit: variants
hint: Fair balance is required on every served product-claim variant, not a sample.
explain: All 500 must carry intact ISI. There is no compliant threshold below 100 percent, because any single served variant without ISI is itself a fair-balance violation regardless of how the other 499 look.
:::

:::predict
prompt: After 72 hours of testing, a DCO engine strips ISI from 20 of 500 variants before anyone catches it. What is the compliant-variant rate, and is it acceptable?
answer: 96

unit: percent
hint: Compliant-variant rate is variants with intact ISI divided by total variants.
explain: 480 / 500 = 0.96, a 96 percent compliant-variant rate. The target is 100 percent, so 96 percent is a live compliance failure: 20 non-compliant variants are serving, which is exactly why ISI is locked as non-optimizable before testing starts.
:::

# MLR approval is a state, and automation can invalidate it

MLR approval attaches to a specific asset with specific copy. The instant automation alters approved content, the approval no longer covers what is serving, and MLR must be re-triggered. This is the most dangerous drift in the whole pipeline because it is silent: DCO that mutates a headline, or pacing automation that swaps in a copy variant, can invalidate approval without a single human noticing.

So the operator runs two non-negotiable rules. First, only MLR-approved creative flights, full stop. Second, any automated post-launch change to an approved claim or ISI asset re-starts MLR review on that asset. The practical defense is to keep an approved backup asset ready, because the moment approval lapses on the live creative, delivery on it must stop.

:::quiz
question: A DCO system rewrites the headline on an MLR-approved product-claim ad mid-flight to lift engagement. What is the correct operator response?
- Nothing, DCO output inherits the original MLR approval
- Treat it as a new asset and re-trigger MLR review before it keeps serving
- Let it run and note the change in the post-campaign report
- Only re-review if engagement also drops
answer: 1
hint: Approval is tied to the exact approved copy, not to the campaign in general.
explain: Any automated change to an approved claim or ISI asset invalidates the approval and re-triggers MLR. DCO output does not inherit approval, the change is not a reporting footnote, and the trigger is the content change itself, not the engagement result.
:::

# The enforcement is live, and the pause has a budget cost

This is not theoretical risk. FDA's OPDP issued at least 9 untitled letters and 1 warning letter in Q1 2026 alone (with more by Q2, plus over 30 compounder warning letters on semaglutide and tirzepatide), spread across oncology and hematology (Brukinsa, Vonjo, Anktiva), weight-loss and metabolic (Wegovy, Ozempic), autoimmune and neurologic (Vyvgart Hytrulo, Neffy), and immunology and dermatology (Tremfya) rather than concentrated in any one area, including a 2026 challenge to Novo Nordisk over a "Live Lighter" execution that overstated emotional outcomes. The operator's reason to care is concrete: enforcement forces a creative pause, and a pause inside a fixed flight is unspent or at-risk budget against the only KPI that funds renewal, cost per incremental script.

Work the cost like any other pacing constraint. If a flight has a fixed budget and FDA action freezes the only compliant creative for part of the window, the at-risk budget is the flight budget scaled by the paused fraction of the flight. On a $900,000 twelve-week flight, a three-week forced pause exposes $900,000 times $3/12 = \$225{,}000$, which is why a pre-cleared backup asset is a budget instrument, not a nicety.

# DTC privacy: HIPAA-safe by construction, policed by OCR

The DTC motion adds a second regulator. Reaching patients and consumers must stay HIPAA-safe under HHS Office for Civil Rights (OCR): no protected health information (PHI) in the buy and no targeting on sensitive or behavioral-health conditions. The operator builds DTC audiences from condition-contextual and privacy-safe signals, never from anything that ties an identifiable person to a diagnosis.

The trader's job here is to keep the targeting and measurement plumbing clean by construction. That means contextual and consented audience inputs, no PHI flowing into the DSP, and downstream script reconciliation handled de-identified inside a HIPAA clean room rather than via any user-level health pixel. Compliant DTC is an architecture decision made before launch, not a disclaimer added after.

:::callout warning
A recurring operator pain point: "the most dangerous compliance failures happen in post-launch optimization" when automation alters approved content without re-triggering MLR, and DCO platforms strip the ISI block as a "low-performing element," creating a compliance fire drill mid-flight. On social such as Reddit, teams also report ISI must be pinned in-post (scrolling ISI / PI), never relegated to comments. Audit the live creative for ISI integrity and MLR coverage continuously, do not assume launch-day approval still holds at week three.
:::

:::quiz
question: For a DTC pharma campaign, which targeting approach keeps the buy HIPAA-safe under OCR?
- Build audiences from users who searched a specific diagnosis and matched by PHI
- Use condition-contextual and consented signals with no PHI in the buy
- Retarget patients who viewed a sensitive behavioral-health condition page
- Pass diagnosis data into the DSP for tighter targeting
answer: 1
hint: HIPAA-safe means no PHI and no sensitive-condition targeting reaches the buy.
explain: Condition-contextual, consented targeting with no PHI is the HIPAA-safe construction. The other options all push protected health information or sensitive-condition signals into the targeting layer, which is exactly what OCR scrutiny is designed to catch.
:::
